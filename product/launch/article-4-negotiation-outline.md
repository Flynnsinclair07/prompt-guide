# Article #4 — Outline

## Hook (3 paragraphs, ~250 words)

**Para 1:** Open with the specific failure mode. You got the offer. The base is $15K below what comparable roles at comparable companies pay. You opened ChatGPT, asked it to write a counter-offer email, got back a confident three-paragraph reply that started with "I am thrilled to accept this offer with some adjustments..." and ended with "I look forward to your prompt response on these matters." You sent it. Hiring manager went cold for two days. When they responded, the offer stayed at the original number with a tone that signaled: this person doesn't know how to do this.

**Para 2:** Why ChatGPT negotiation scripts fail. The model is trained on corporate negotiation playbooks — most of which are old, generic, and written for senior executives at Fortune 500s. Apply them to an early-career counter and you get the same problem: language that signals "I learned this from a script." Hiring managers can tell instantly. Worse, the standard AI counter-offer comes across as either too aggressive (demanding) or too apologetic (undercutting yourself). Almost never the calibrated middle.

**Para 3:** The fix isn't to skip ChatGPT. ChatGPT is genuinely useful for negotiation prep — it can research comp ranges, structure your asks, anticipate objections, draft the actual email language, and stress-test your justifications. The fix is to use it for the *prep* layer, then write the actual counter in your voice. This guide walks through how to do that without producing the AI-flavored, bridge-burning version most people send.

## Section: Why ChatGPT negotiation scripts sound aggressive (or worse, scripted)

Two reasons:

1. **The training corpus is dominated by "how to negotiate" articles** written by negotiation consultants, FAANG-level engineers, or executive coaches — all aimed at audiences with leverage. The model produces output assuming you have a competing offer, a senior title, or 8+ years of experience. Apply that tone to an entry-level offer and you sound presumptuous.

2. **Counter-offer emails are highly formula-driven in the training data.** "I am thrilled to receive..." / "Based on market research..." / "I would like to propose..." / "Please let me know if this works." The model has read 50,000 versions of these and produces them on autopilot. The hiring manager has read 50,000 too. They recognize the structure as soon as they see it.

The other issue: **negotiation is relational, not transactional.** The hiring manager you're negotiating with is the same person you'll work for next month. A counter-offer that's calibrated correctly opens a conversation. A counter-offer that sounds scripted shuts one down — even if the numbers are reasonable. The relational damage costs you the soft asks (start date, vacation, signing bonus) that don't even cost the company anything to grant.

## Section: The four failure modes to watch for

1. **Aggressive opening with no warmth.** "I am writing to discuss the compensation offered..." — sounds like a complaint letter, not a negotiation. Real counters open with appreciation first, then the ask.
2. **Demanding language disguised as politeness.** "I would like to request..." / "I propose the following..." — these phrases sound polite but read as ultimatums when stacked. Real negotiation language uses softer framings: "would you be open to..." / "if there's flexibility on..." / "I wanted to ask about..."
3. **Justifications that overreach.** ChatGPT will pad your justification with anything it can — "given my unique skill set," "based on the strategic value I'll bring." Most of this is generic and unprovable. Real justifications are narrow and specific: one market data point + one thing about you that supports the number.
4. **Ultimatum framing.** "This number works for me" / "I'd need at least X to accept." Real counters leave the door open for the company to come back: "I'd love to make this work — is there room to get closer to X?" The latter signals you want the job. The former signals you might walk.

## Section: The right process, step by step

### Step 1: Research the comp range before you ask ChatGPT for anything.

Open Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Comprehensive.io, or your industry's specific comp database. Find the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile for your role/level/location. Write them down in a doc — not in ChatGPT. The numbers need to come from real data, not an LLM estimate. (ChatGPT will hallucinate comp ranges. Always check.)

You're looking for: where does the offer fall in the range? If 50th percentile, your counter targets 75th. If 25th, your counter targets 50th.

### Step 2: Use ChatGPT to stress-test your justification, not to write the email.

Once you have a target number, give ChatGPT the offer details and your background, and prompt:

> Given this offer of $X for a [role] in [location], and my background of [experience], play the role of the hiring manager. What would be the strongest objection to a counter of $Y? Generate the 3 most likely pushback questions and the underlying concerns behind them.

This flips the model from "help me write a counter" to "help me anticipate the pushback." Now you know what objections are coming. You can prepare answers — in your own words — before you send the counter.

### Step 3: Draft the counter yourself first. Then have ChatGPT critique it.

Write a 3-4 sentence counter-offer in your own voice. Don't worry about polish. Then paste it into ChatGPT and prompt:

> Critique this counter-offer email. Identify any phrases that sound aggressive, scripted, or AI-generated. Suggest tighter language for each. Do not rewrite the whole email — just flag the problems.

This keeps your voice at the center while using the model to catch the failures you'd miss. The output will surface the 2-3 phrases that sound off, and you can fix them yourself.

### Step 4: Test the counter against the "would my friend send this?" filter.

Read the counter out loud. Would you send this exact wording to a friend who was hiring you for a side project? If no — too formal, too scripted, too demanding — rewrite. The counter should sound like a thoughtful person asking, not a candidate executing a playbook.

### Step 5: Send in writing, not on a call.

This is the universal rule of negotiation. A written counter gives the hiring manager time to escalate internally (most counters require manager approval), removes you from the spot of having to respond live, and locks the numbers in writing. Even if the recruiter calls you to "discuss," respond with "I'd love to put my thoughts in writing so we have a clear record — I'll follow up via email today."

### Step 6: After the counter lands, wait at least 24 hours before following up.

ChatGPT will tempt you to follow up faster. Don't. Hiring managers need internal approval cycles. Silence in the first 24-48 hours is not bad news — it's process. The Day 4 follow-up is where the line is: if you haven't heard back by then, a short "wanted to make sure my message landed — happy to discuss" is appropriate.

## Section: When to stop using ChatGPT and just write it yourself

Three pieces of negotiation work you should do yourself, every time:

**1. The opening sentence.** Whatever phrase opens your counter sets the entire tone. Generic ChatGPT openers ("I'm excited about this opportunity...") signal you used AI. Your opening should reference something specific from the offer call, the company, or the role — something only you know.

**2. The reason you want the job.** If the negotiation goes back and forth, you'll need to reaffirm your interest. A generated "I'm enthusiastic about contributing to..." sounds hollow. Write the one-sentence real reason yourself and use that as your anchor.

**3. The walkaway line.** If you're genuinely prepared to decline at the original number, the language has to be yours. No script handles this well — every candidate's walkaway is unique to their situation. Write it once, in your voice, before you ever send the counter.

## Section: The short version

ChatGPT salary-negotiation scripts sound scripted because the training data is dominated by aggressive corporate playbooks written for executives, not early-career candidates. The fix: research real comp ranges yourself, use ChatGPT to stress-test objections and critique your draft (not to write it), keep the actual email in your voice, send in writing, wait 24+ hours before following up.

The goal is a counter the hiring manager reads and thinks "this is a reasonable person making a reasonable ask" — not "this person learned negotiation from a YouTube video." A bridge-burning counter costs you the relationship; a calibrated counter opens it.
