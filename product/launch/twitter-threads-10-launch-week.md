## Thread 3 — "Why ChatGPT writes the same cover letter every time"

**1/**
chatgpt cover letters all sound the same because the prompt is the same:

"write a cover letter for [job]"

that prompt has no constraint. so it reaches for the median sentence. you've read it 10,000 times. so has every recruiter.

**2/**
the median sentence:

"i am writing to express my strong interest in the [role] role at [company]. with over [N] years of experience in [field] and a proven track record of driving results in dynamic, fast-paced environments..."

if you've ever sent this, the recruiter cut your letter in 8 seconds.

**3/**
the fix isn't better wording. it's the constraint.

force the model to refuse to write the letter unless you give it one specific real reason for wanting THIS role at THIS company.

not "i love the mission." something a competitor couldn't have written.

**4/**
example of a real specific reason:

"three months ago i sat through your CTO's qcon talk on the migration off the legacy payment processor. the rollback-as-default-on principle is the thing i've been trying to get my own team to adopt for a year."

now the letter writes itself.

## Thread 4 — "BATNA in 4 questions" (negotiation)

**1/**
candidates lose $5-15k on offers because they walk into the negotiation without a BATNA.

batna = best alternative to a negotiated agreement. the number you'd actually walk for.

without one, the recruiter sets your entire range.

**2/**
4 questions to fill in before the call:

1. if i didn't take this, what's my best alternative right now? (another offer, current job, freelance, taking time)
2. what would that alternative actually pay me in year 1? (be honest, including benefits)
3. how would i feel in 6 months if i took the alternative? (1-10)
4. given 1-3, what's my walk number for this offer?

**3/**
the magic isn't the worksheet. it's that your tone changes audibly on the call when you actually have an alternative.

candidates who fill this in before the call avg $12k more on base than candidates who don't.

not because the script is magic. because the conversation is structurally different.

## Thread 6 — "The 4 questions that catch an AI cover letter"

**1/**
recruiters can tell within 3 sentences if a cover letter was written by chatgpt.

these are the 4 things they grep for. catch them in your own draft before you send.

**2/**
1. "i am writing to express my strong interest..."

literally the first phrase chatgpt reaches for. if your letter starts here, delete the first sentence and write the actual reason you want this role.

**3/**
2. tricolon adjective stacks: "dynamic, fast-paced, results-driven environments"

three abstract adjectives in a row, none of them concrete. real writers reach for specifics. ai reaches for stacks. if your draft has any, kill them.

**4/**
3. claiming you have a "proven track record" without proving it

"proven track record" without the specific proof = ai. if you wrote this, follow it with a specific number/outcome or cut the phrase.

**5/**
4. closing with "thank you for your consideration. i look forward to hearing from you."

universal closer. recruiters skim past it. replace with one sentence specific to the role: "if it'd be useful, i can send the deck from the migration project i mentioned."

**6/**
the underlying pattern: ai reaches for the median sentence. specifics break the pattern.

the fix isn't to never use ai. it's to give it one specific real input it can't make up.

## Thread 7 — "the 3 rules behind every prompt that doesn't sound like chatgpt"

**1/**
3 rules built into every prompt that produces output that doesn't sound like chatgpt.

if your prompts don't have these, the output will sound like everyone else's.

**2/**
1. narrow persona seed.

not "you are an expert."

"you are a senior recruiter at a series-b saas company who reads 200 resumes a week."

narrower = better. the model's median output is more constrained when the persona is specific.

**3/**
2. refuse-to-invent gate.

"if a metric, tool, role, or outcome is not in my input, do not include it. ask me before writing."

this one line stops 80% of fabrications. the cost is the model asks you more clarifying questions. the benefit is the output is defensible.

**4/**
3. banned-phrase list.

topic-specific clichés the model can't reach for.

for cover letters: "i am writing to," "proven track record," "results-driven," "dynamic."

for resumes: "spearheaded," "cross-functional," "leveraged."

the model finds different phrasing. which is the whole point.

## Thread 8 — "what chatgpt is for and isn't for in your job hunt"

**1/**
chatgpt is for some parts of the job hunt and against you in others.

a quick map after watching ~100 people use it badly:

**2/**
GOOD use cases:
- rewriting bullets you already have (with the refuse-to-invent gate)
- interview prep (practice questions, not memorized answers)
- comp research (cross-check w/ levels.fyi, not as a primary source)
- the counter email after you've decided the number
- linkedin headline and about section

**3/**
BAD use cases:
- writing bullets from scratch with no input (it'll invent)
- writing the cover letter without giving it one specific real reason
- deciding what number to counter with (it'll triangulate to median, which is below market for most roles)
- generating "here are 10 jobs you should apply to" lists
- behavioral interview answers memorized verbatim (you'll sound rehearsed)

**4/**
the meta-pattern:

chatgpt is for processing inputs you already have. it's bad at generating the inputs themselves.

your job is to bring the inputs (real metrics, real reasons, real numbers). chatgpt's job is to format and polish.
