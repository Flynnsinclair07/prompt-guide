## Issue 2 — Wed June 3, 2026

**Subject:** the one line that fixes 80% of cover letters

**Preview text:** specifically, the banned-phrase list — and why a single rule beats five rounds of editing.

---

hey,

Most cover letters fail in the first sentence. Not the second, not the third — the first. Recruiters skim the opener; if it sounds templated, they're already done.

The default ChatGPT cover letter opens with: *"I am writing to express my strong interest in the [Position] role at [Company]."*

Every recruiter has read that exact sentence ten thousand times. It communicates one thing: I used a template.

The fix is one line in your prompt. Add this:

> Banned opening phrases — do not use any of these:
> "I am writing to," "I came across," "As a [title]," "I would like to apply," "Dear Hiring Manager."
> Replace with a specific opener tied to my real reason for applying.

That single line forces the model away from the median. The opener it produces will instead be something like "*Three months ago I attended your CTO's talk at QCon, and the way she framed internal tooling as a product changed how I structured my own platform team.*" — specific, unfakeable, and impossible to confuse with a template.

The same trick works for resumes (banned: "results-driven," "proven track record"), interview answers (banned: "the key takeaway was"), and salary counters (banned: "I would like to request").

One rule. Stops 80% of the AI-tell.

## Issue 3 — Wed June 10, 2026

**Subject:** BATNA in 4 questions — most candidates skip this

**Preview text:** the negotiation move that makes the actual difference. Three minutes, no spreadsheet.

---

hey,

If you're going to negotiate an offer in the next 6 months, the single most useful thing to do before the call is figure out your BATNA.

BATNA = Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement. Plain English: what you'd actually do if this offer falls through. Not your hope, not your aspiration — the specific, real, available option you'd take.

Most candidates skip this because it sounds like a business-school concept. It's not. It's the difference between a counter-offer that the company takes seriously and one they nod through.

The 4-question version, run before any offer call:

1. If this offer goes away today, what do I actually do? (Stay in current role / take a specific other live offer / wait on a near-offer / search from unemployment with N months of runway.)
2. What's the comp value of that path?
3. How does that compare to this offer — higher, similar, lower, much lower?
4. Am I confident I'd take it, or am I bluffing myself?

The answers determine how aggressively you can counter. BATNA higher than the offer = real walk-away leverage. BATNA much lower = counter from market data and outcomes, not from leverage. The classic mistake is acting like you have leverage when you don't — or hiding leverage you actually have.

Run those 4 questions on your own situation today. Even if you don't have an offer yet, the answers calibrate everything you do next.

## Issue 4 — Wed June 17, 2026

**Subject:** the post-output validation step (no one does this)

**Preview text:** the single change that stopped my ChatGPT writing from sounding like ChatGPT.

---

hey,

There's a step nobody adds to their AI prompts that does more for output quality than any clever phrasing trick.

It's this: after the model writes its draft, instruct it to re-read its own output and flag anything that violates the rules in the prompt.

Specifically, add this at the end of your prompt:

> AFTER you write the draft, re-read it as the recipient (recruiter / interviewer / customer / etc.) and flag:
> 1. Any banned phrase that slipped through.
> 2. Any claim or number you can't trace to my input.
> 3. Any sentence that sounds generic enough to apply to any candidate.
> List what you found, then offer corrections.

Why it works: the model is much better at catching its own slips than at avoiding them in the first place. The first pass is generative; the second pass is editorial. Splitting those into two passes — in the same prompt — gives you both halves.

I run this step on every prompt I write. It catches roughly 70% of the AI-tells that would otherwise survive to the final draft. The other 30% you catch on your own read.

The bundle's prompts have this validation step baked in. The free versions on the site have lighter versions of it. Try it on whatever prompt you're using today — even ChatGPT's free defaults get noticeably better.
